Extracting and reconstructing knowledge
in materials science literature using large

Knowledge reconstruction is required for both existing and new material discovery. However, this
process faces signiﬁcant challenges, especially in ensuring the completeness and logical consistency
of extracted information. We present a generalized method that can be used to reconstruct knowledge
from inorganic science literature regarding synthetic routes and properties. Using a one-prompt
design, a comprehensive dataset was built with the GPT-4 model, which was subsequently used to
ﬁne-tune four large language models (LLMs), including LLaMA3-8B-instruct (Llama), Gemma-7B
(Gemma), Phi3-mini-128k-instruct (Phi), GPT3.5-turbo-1106 (GPT). The ﬁne-tuned models
demonstrated a strong capability in reconstructing synthetic routes for selective catalytic reduction
(SCR). The information extraction (IE) process achieved high performance on the task of material
synthesis route extraction, with a precision of 0.928, a recall of 0.957, and an F1 score of 0.962. The
ﬁne-tuned models were then used for large-scale IE, extracting 48,925 entities from 2205 articles. Due
to the JSON structure of the initial GPT-4-constructed dataset, the extracted data was stored in the
same format, providing a valuable resource for further research. The four ﬁne-tuned LLMs were
transferred to ﬁve other domains, including lithium-ion batteries (Li-ion), hydrogen evolution reaction
(HER), oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), oxygen evolution reaction (OER), and methanol steam
reforming (MSR), to test the generalized performance of the ﬁne-tuned LLMs. The results show that the
ﬁne-tuned LLMs have high transferability across domains and can be applied to a wide range of
materials science tasks. Ultimately, a comprehensive knowledge graph was constructed by
integrating multiple entities (nodes) including article metadata, catalysts, preparation methods, raw
materials, synthesis steps, synthesis conditions and performance metrics. These nodes are
interconnected through various relational edges (such as synthesis method, synthesis action,
temperature, pressure, time, atmosphere, equipment, conversion, selectivity, activity) that represent
explicit physicochemical dependencies, procedural parameters, and performance correlations. This
structured framework enables the efﬁcient exploration of intricate relationships within materials
science literature, thereby facilitating the rapid identiﬁcation of high-performance catalysts and the
data-driven optimization of synthesis.
The synthesis of new materials is the key to promoting scientiﬁc and
technological progress1. However, materials synthesis presents signiﬁcant
challenges. The complexity of materials science requires that researchers
possess extensive foundational knowledge in physics and chemistry, as well
as proﬁciency in advanced experimental and computational methods.
Moreover, materials are inﬂuenced by various factors during synthesis
process, such as temperature, pressure, and chemical environment. For
instance, SCR catalysts are commonly prepared via impregnation, pre-
cipitation, and sol-gel methods, each differing in raw materials, synthesis
procedures, and reaction conditions. Minor deviations, such as subtle

Data-driven artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) has developed rapidly and
played an important role in various ﬁelds3. The advantagesof AI technology
in materials design are becoming increasingly prominent, and the integra-
tion of AI and big data is being called the “fourth industrial revolution”4,5.
These capabilities are primarily driven by machine learning (ML), enabling
data-driven pattern recognition and predictive modeling. Various ML
techniques, including regression models6–8, classiﬁcation models9,10, neural
networks11, reinforcement learning12, and graph neural networks13, have
been widely applied in materials development such as lithium batteries14,15,
metal-organic frameworks16,17, chemical reactions18, and materials design19.
The training of ML models relies on a vast amount of reliable and nor-
malized scientiﬁc data, which are mainly derived from existing databases,
experimental or computational results from researchers’ laboratories, and
scientiﬁc literature published by other researchers20,21. In particular, scien-
tiﬁc literature usually contains rich information on detailed experimental
procedures, conditions, and observations22. In addition, these sources
contain the latest peer-reviewed research results and innovative methods,
with high reliability and credibility. Therefore, effectively mining and
organizinginformation fromthematerialsliteratureiscriticalforadvancing
data-driven materials research. Unfortunately, manually extracting and
reconstructing knowledge from scientiﬁc literature is a labor-intensive
process, which hinders the efﬁcient acquisition of valuable insights.
In recent years, natural language processing (NLP) techniques have
been successfully applied to extract scientiﬁc literature data in the ﬁelds of
chemistry23,24, medicine, biology25, and materials science26, mining a large
amountofexcellentdata.TheapplicationofNLPtechniqueshasaccelerated
ontology development in materials science. Bayerlein et al.27 employed an
NLP-driven ontology development approach to provide a standardized
semanticframeworkfortheﬁeld ofmaterialssciencemicroscopy,effectively
addressing issues of terminology ambiguity and retrieval efﬁciency, and
advancing the application of FAIR (Findability, Accessibility, Interoper-
ability, Reusability) data management principles in experimental science.
Traditionally, specialized NLP methods including dictionary matching,
rules, ML and deep learning models have been employed to solve this issue
related to IE and semantic standardization. Liu et al.28 utilized rule-based
and domain dictionaries framework to extract valuable domain knowledge
from abstracts of scientiﬁc literature, with a particular focus on Ni-based
single crystal superalloys. Li et al.23 designed rules to extract catalyst
synthesis data from scientiﬁc literature, speciﬁcally focusing on Pd-based
methanolsteamreforming.TheextracteddatawasusedtotrainMLmodels,
which were designed to recommend optimal synthesis routes. Liu et al. and
Cruse et al. carried out extensive research, training BiLSTM-CRF models
based on transformer architectures, to extract synthesis procedures for
solution-based inorganicmaterials29,and gold nanoparticles30.Additionally,
ML models such as MatSciBERT31 and ChemBERT32 have been trained on
annotated data to extract speciﬁc chemical information. These approaches
canbelabor-intensiveandnecessitateexpertiseincoding,computerscience,
and data science. Furthermore, they are less generalizable, requiring rede-
sign or training when the target changes.
LLMs have demonstrated excellent capabilities in NLP to extract
information from literature, understand and generate chemical data.
Jablonka et al.24 ﬁne-tuned the GPT-3 model to perform tasks such as
molecular property prediction, reaction yield estimation, and even inverse
molecular design through natural language question. Zheng et al.33 used
prompt engineering techniques to guide ChatGPT in mining the scientiﬁc
literature for the synthesis of metal-organic frameworks with information on
metal sources, linkers, solvents, reaction temperatures, and times. Polak
et al.34 used conversational ChatGPT to extract materials properties such as
the critical cooling rates of metallic glasses and yield strength of high entropy
alloys in the form of the triplet material, value, unit. Walker et al.35 proposed a
method using GPT-3 model to extract structured multi-step seed-mediated
growth procedures for gold nanorods from scientiﬁc text by utilizing a for-
mulated synthetic template containing seed solution, growth solution and
generated nanorods. Pei et al.36 demonstrated that LLMs can expedite the
materials design process and enable the inclusion of holistic criteria in the
domain of alloy design and manufacturing. Dagdelen et al.37 also used LLMs
(GPT-3, Llama-2) to perform IE tasks: dopant-host systems, metal-organic
frameworks, and general materials information. Their approach focused on
the automatic extraction of entities such as materials, dopant-host relation-
ships, structure components, phases, morphologies, and applications.
Venugopal et al.38,39 constructed MatKG and MatKG-2 knowledge graphs by
extracting information such as materials, properties, applications, and char-
acterization methods from abstract of literature, using BERT-based approa-
ches for MatKG and LLMs (Llama2 7B and Bloom 1B/7B) for MatKG-2.
Previous studies have successfully leveraged LLMs or neural network models
to extract entities and relationships. Few studies37 have addressed the com-
prehensive extraction of material names, synthesis details, material perfor-
mance, source metadata, and information with strong logical relationships
from the full text of scientiﬁc literature. Existing materials science informa-
tion extraction studies focus on structured data acquisition37,40–42, but differ
from this work in LLM strategy, extracted information, cross-domain cap-
ability, and output format. These distinctions highlight the novelty of our
universal framework for inorganic catalytic materials.

hensive knowledge reconstruction from materials science literature. This
approach enables detailed analysis of each step and condition within

The one-shot prompt (described in Method) was designed to instruct
GPT-4 to extract the synthesis route from the paragraph describing the
material synthesis. Using the prompt, synthesis paragraphs were input to
GPT-4 model, which extracted corresponding synthesis information, detail-
ing each step, conditions, and materials involved. Three domain experts
manually reviewed the GPT-4 generated content according to the output
template, corrected the identiﬁed errors, and resolved discrepancies through
discussion and negotiation to ensure annotation consistency and reliability.
This iterative workﬂow, involving synthesis paragraph input, synthesis route
generation, and manual review, improved prompt design with each iteration.
With this methodology, a work-cleaned dataset capturing detailed and
accurate material synthesis routes was created, which was used to ﬁne-tune
LLMs. The work-cleaned dataset consists of two key components: synthesis
paragraphs and the corresponding synthesis routes information. Each synth-
esis paragraph describes the detailed procedures and conditions for material
synthesis. The dataset comprises a total of 156 synthesis paragraphs, con-
taining 1330 sentences and 35855 tokens. Additionally, the dataset includes
156 synthesis routes, detailing raw materials, products, methods, and relevant
information for each step, and encompasses 6337 entities. The dataset provides
a comprehensive and structured representation of the synthesis process.

The described synthetic template training dataset was used to ﬁne-tune the
LLMs to reproduce synthetic templates in unstructured text. The para-
metersforthefourLLMs’ﬁne-tuningprocessweredescribedinMethods.In
this study, the Llama, Gemma, and Phi models were ﬁne-tuned using

usagearound23GB.FortheGPTmodel,online ﬁne-tuningwasperformed,
which required ~10 min without consuming local computational resources.
The detailed procedure for the ﬁne-tuning process is provided in the Sup-
plementary Methods. Fine-tuning improves on few-shot learning by
trainingonmanymoreexamplesthancanﬁtintheprompt,achievingbetter
results on a wide number of tasks. Once ﬁne-tuned, the model can generate
responses without examples in the prompt.

The sequence (logic) of experimental synthesis steps (actions) plays a
crucial role, as it can greatly affect the properties and quality of the resulting
material. Chemical reactions involved in different steps of the synthesis
process are strongly dependent on the operation order. Incorrect sequen-
cing can lead to incomplete reactions or the formation of undesirable by-
products. The physical structure and properties of the material can be sig-
niﬁcantly inﬂuenced by the sequence of operations. The sequence of
synthesis operations is crucial to the success of material synthesis, affecting
everything from chemical reactions to reproducibility and safety. By
understanding the sequence of synthesis actions, experimenters can more
accurately replicate the process and achieve consistent material properties.
The evaluation of the synthesis sequence provides invaluable insights for
researchers.

Extraction of material properties
The material synthesis process directly affects the microstructure of the
material, and these microstructures determine the macroscopic properties
of the material. Material properties can also be extracted using LLMs. One-
shot prompt methods were employed to extract material properties such as
activity, selectivity, and conversion of SCR catalysts. In the prompt, material
properties, including conversion, selectivity, activity, and reaction condi-
tions, were explicitly listed for extraction. Also, to minimize illusions, the
model was instructed to output “not mentioned” for materials that did not
have that property. Examples of one-shot prompts are in Supplementary
Methods. Figure 8 shows an example of the inputs and outputs of LLM for
extracting material properties and reaction conditions.
Large-scale data extraction
In our approach, the titles were extracted from the Extensible Markup
Language (XML) nodes and then compared with a predeﬁned list of titles
related to material synthesis, such as “catalyst preparation” and “materials
synthesis”. To ensure robustness, the extracted titles were converted to
lowercaseandanyleadingortrailingwhitespacewasstripped,standardizing
the titles for comparison. Next, the list of extracted titles was iterated and
each title was compared with the predeﬁned titles. If a match was found, the
regular expression patterns were applied to the corresponding paragraph
content to identify paragraphs discussing material synthesis. This process
allowed for the effective extraction of relevant paragraphs from the litera-
ture. From this process, 2205 articles were identiﬁed containing paragraphs
directly related to synthesis.
Following ﬁne-tuning, the models are not required prompts in the
input. They can directly process synthesis-related text to produce structured
synthesis routes. Using the ﬁne-tuned models, we performed large-scale
extraction of synthesis information from these 2205 articles in the materials
science literature. The models automatically generated structured synthesis
routes and ﬂowcharts from input synthesis paragraphs. Figure 9 shows an
example of automatic ﬂowchart generation from a paragraph45. These
routes were processed and saved in a standardized JSON format, facilitating
subsequent research applications and analyses. The standardized data can
be accessed at https://github.com/Shaoruisun/LLMs-MatLitRecon.
As a result, a total of 48925 entities were successfully extracted,
encompassing a wide range of information critical to understanding
material synthesis processes. These entities include 3715 product entities,
which represent the materials or compounds synthesized; 3186 preparation
method entities, detailing the various methodologies employed; 3011 raw
material entities, identifying the starting materials used in synthesis; 19102
synthesis action entities, which describe the speciﬁc steps involved in the
synthesis processes; and 23626 preparation condition entities, outlining the
conditions under which the synthesis was carried out, such as temperature,
pressure, and time. The extraction of such a comprehensive set of entities
enables a detailed and structured representation of the synthesis knowledge,
facilitating further analysis and the potential for automation in material
discovery and optimization.
Models transfer
Four LLMs, Llama, Gemma, Phi, and GPT, are ﬁne-tuned to focus on
materials science literature in the ﬁeld of SCR. The ﬁne-tuned models are
intended to enhance their capability in extracting complex material
synthesis routes and related information. Subsequently, these ﬁne-tuned
models were transferred to four other different domains, namely the HER,
MSR, ORR, OER, and Lit-ion domains, in order to test their generalizability
in different domains.
In each domain, 20 articles were randomly selected from the corre-
sponding corpus. The DOIs of the test set articles are in Supplementary
Table 7-11. Knowledge was extracted from the literature in each of the ﬁve
domains using the ﬁne-tuned models. Similarly, ﬁve tasks focusing on
materials synthesis were performed: product extraction, raw material
identiﬁcation, synthesis method identiﬁcation, extraction of synthesis steps,
and generalization of synthesis conditions. The test results are presented in
Fig. 10, with detailed results summarized in Supplementary Data (Supple-
mentary Tables 12-15). As shown, the Llama, Gemma, and GPT models
have excellent transferability, and the Phi model demonstrates slightly
weaker performance. The results show that although different models
perform distinctively on different domains and tasks, the ﬁne-tuned models
generally have high cross-domain transfer capabilities and are able to adapt
to diverse materials science tasks.

Theknowledgegraphisastructuredrepresentationofinformationthat
capturesrelationshipsbetweenentities,typicallythroughanetworkofnodes
(representing entities) and edges (representing relationships). Knowledge
graphs are widely used to organize and integrate vast amounts of complex,
interconnected data, facilitating both human understanding and machine
processing. By leveraging triples in the form of (h,r,t), where “h” represents
the head entity, “r” represents the relationship, and “t” represents the tail
entity, knowledge graphs offer a formalized way to represent information
through logical connections. The knowledge graph consists of a total of
26400 nodes and 55338 relationships. As illustrated in Fig. 11, the graph
displays 1000 sets of relationships, offering a representative subset of the
overall structure.
The knowledge graph was constructed with a hierarchical, catalyst-
centered logic, as illustrated in the provided Fig. 12. On the left side, the
graph presents a top-level structure where each catalyst, such as Ce/TiO₂,
serves as the central node. Directly connected to this node are ﬁrst-level
entities, including raw materials used for synthesis, metadata such as lit-
erature sources (e.g., DOI, publication title, publication date, and other
relevant data), key performance metrics (e.g., conversion), and general
synthesis methods. This top-level view offers a concise summary of all
primary information associated with the catalyst, allowing users to quickly
grasp its background, preparation technique, and main application results.
On the right side, the graph expands into a more detailed, multi-level
structure by decomposing the synthesis route into a series of sequential
experimental steps. The central catalyst node is linked to a synthesis step
node, whichaggregates all individual operations involvedin the preparation

Datasets
The ﬁrst stage aims to acquire a corpus of articles relevant to the topic. The
relevant articles for constructing the training and testing datasets of this
model were collected from the Elsevier database, under text and data-
mining agreements. Several reasons make these journal articles particularly
suitable for NLP workﬂows48. These articles can be downloaded and stored
locally; they are digitized rather than scanned, at least when they are recent
enough, so they can be processed without errors that might arise from
having to convert images to text; they can be tracked via DOIs, so the data
extracted from them can be referenced. Elsevier articles were downloaded
via the ScienceDirect Search Application Programming Interface (API),
which is provided by Elsevier for text and data mining purposes. We con-
structed text mining programs to identify and extract articles containing
SCR syntheses. First, we repeatedly submitted the query text for “selective
catalytic reduction” in the search bar of the Elsevier homepage via a Python
web-scraping tool and recorded the responses on each page. Then, we
extracted the metadata of the articles from the responses, including titles,
author names, URL links, and DOI information. If a DOI or URL was
provided, it was used to access the content of the relevant articles. Based on
the extracted DOIs or URLs, the content of the article in XML format was
further retrieved from the Elsevier server. Given that articles prior to 2000
were presented in PDF format, we limited the publication years to
2000−2023 when requesting articles from Elsevier, and downloaded a total
of 12136 articles. The corpus contained 2305 articles with information on
catalyst preparation paragraph. From these, 156 paragraph were selected for
work-cleaned dataset construction based on two criteria: achieving a
balanced distribution across publication years and ensuring a diverse
representation of preparation methods.
To ensure high relevance to the research topic, articles containing
speciﬁc keywords in their titles were retrieved using the same approach,
which automatically downloaded literature from ﬁve additional categories
related to the study. This process covered the period from 2000 to 2023,
resulting in the creation of a comprehensive corpus of inorganic materials
science literature. Speciﬁcally, 3009 papers on HER, 2401 on OER, 3460 on
ORR, 428 on MSR, and 4052 on Li-ion batteries were downloaded. We
developed an automated method using regular expressions to extract para-
graphs discussing “preparation catalysts”, “preparation materials”, “synthesis
catalysts”, and “synthesis materials” from a large corpus of XML literature.

The Output template of GPT-4
In recent years, the management and sharing of research data have
become increasingly important in the materials science community. The
FAIR principles have been widely recognized as fundamental guidelines
for making research data more valuable and usable49,50. Applying these
principles to materials synthesis information is critical to enable efﬁcient
data reuse, facilitate automated data integration, and accelerate data-
driven material discovery. However, synthesis procedures are often
reported in unstructured and inconsistent formats, limiting their acces-
sibility and reusability. Following these principles, we introduced a
structured template for inorganic materials synthesis routes, using a
machine-readable JSON format. This approach ensures compatibility
with the FAIR principles and promotes standardized data sharing within
the community.
The structure and content of the synthesis are shown in Fig. 16. The
synthesis routes are stored as JSON documents, which contain ﬁve com-
ponents: products, synthesis methods, raw materials, synthesis steps, and
corresponding synthesis conditions. The products include the name and
denotation of the catalyst. All raw materials, including supports, reagents,
solvents, etc., and their associated volumes (vol), concentrations (con),
ratios, and/or masses were documented. All synthetic actions related to the
synthesis steps were recorded in the precise order they were performed
during the experimental synthesis. Additionally, the conditions associated
with each synthetic action were noted, including temperature, time, pH,
reagents, equipment, and atmospheric environment.
The documents follow a standardized format,with the exception of the
synthesis steps, which differ depending on the speciﬁc requirements of each
study. Unlike other sections, the synthesis steps are not predeﬁned; instead,
they are customized to accurately reﬂect the unique experimental proce-
dures of each individual work. This approach ensures that the synthesis
process is thoroughly documented, capturing all relevant details and var-
iations that are critical for replicating and understanding the synthetic
outcomes.

graph—can enhance domain knowledge and support better contextual
modeling. Third, automatic prompt generation algorithms could be adop-
ted, which dynamically create guiding prompts based on the input
text, reducing reliance on ﬁxed templates. Fourth, for low-frequency terms,

The prompt template for GPT-4 consists of four main sections: role
deﬁnition, task speciﬁcation, input designation and output designation. It is
designed as follows:
RoleDeﬁnition:RoledeﬁnitionisusedtodeﬁnetherolethattheGPT-4
will play during the task and to set the context of the task.
For instance, GPT-4 is instructed to act as a scientiﬁc analyst, “Assume
you are a chemical engineer specializing in the extraction of information
from materials synthesis paragraphs.”. This role determines the perspective
and expertise employed by the model when processing tasks.
TaskSpeciﬁcation:Atthisstage,thetaskswillbedescribedindetail.For
the purpose of extracting materials synthesis route, this section clariﬁes the
objective, such as identifying and summarizing the steps involved in the
synthesis process, including the raw materials, methods, conditions and
reagents used. This ensures that the model understands the scope and focus
of the extraction task. We designed a total of four tasks:
1. Please output the synthesis information for the material basedon the
following paragraph, including the product name, preparation method, raw
materials, and all steps.
2. The steps are operational actions, such as addition, stirring, mixing,
dissolving, transferring, heating, drying, calcining, reducing, activating, and
other similar operations.
3. Output each operation in order, and the conditions associated with
each operation, such as temperature, time, reagents, equipment and
instruments, atmosphere, etc.
4. Please extract the synthesis information separately and output it in
dictionary format.
Input template: The input consists of paragraphs detailing the synthesis
of materials. To illustrate, an example synthesis paragraph is provided within
the prompt template. This paragraph was selected because it contains a
comprehensive synthesis procedure with detailed descriptions of each step.
Output template: In the prompt, the output is the synthesis routes
extracted from the input paragraphs, following the predeﬁned template. At
ﬁrst, the example synthesis routes will be formatted according to the preset
template and presented the required information. Subsequently, the pre-trained
model will autonomously generate synthesis routes based on new input pas-
sages according to the same structural format. The one-shot prompt is shown
in Fig. 17, and the full information is provided in Supplementary Methods.
LLMs
In this study, we ﬁne-tuned several models downloaded from Hugging face
using the LLaMA-Factory framework. To conserve computational resour-
ces, we opted for smaller parameter versions of each LLM. The models
included Gemma (Google), Llama (Meta), and Phi (Microsoft). Addition-
ally, we utilized an online GPT model provided by OpenAI. A detailed
description and hyperparameters of each model are given below.
The comprehensive overview is provided of three open-source LLMs:
Gemma, Llama, and Phi. Each model is designed to cater to various com-
putational needs that enhance their performance on consumer-grade
hardware.
Gemma is a series of lightweight, state-of-the-art open models from
Google, with two versions (2B and 7B). Each version includes a pre-trained
foundational model and an instruction-optimized model, and is capable of
running on consumer-grade hardware without data quantization for up to
8 K tokens. We utilized the 7B parameters pre-trained foundational version.
Meta’s Llama 3, the latest in the Llama series, offers 8B and 70B ver-
sions. Each version has a base model and an instruction-tuned model. We
usethe8Bparameterinstruction-tunedversion(LLaMA3-8B-instruct)with
a context length of 8 K tokens.
Phi-3 is a series of open AI models by Microsoft. We ﬁne-tuned the
Phi-3-mini model, a language model with 3.8 billion parameters and a
context length of 128k tokens.
All three models were ﬁne-tuned using the LLaMA-Factory framework
with the LoRA method. The ﬁne-tuning process parameters involved setting
the training batch size to 1, gradient accumulation steps to 8, learning rate to
3.0e-5, number of training epochs to 24, logging steps to 1, and save steps to 50.
For the GPT model, we utilized the GPT-3.5-turbo-1106 from OpenAI.
The hyperparameters for ﬁne-tuning this model are different from those used
for the Hugging face models. The batch size was set to 1, with a learning rate
multiplier of 1, within the recommended range of 0.1 to 10, and the model
was ﬁne-tuned over 3 epochs. These parameters were chosen to ensure
optimal performance and efﬁcient training for the GPT-3.5-turbo model.
